We follow cloud native design, where the business functionality has segregated into small, independent Microservices.


What we have is cloud-native in the sense that we use microservices and async processing, and we separate reads into Redis. But we still 
share a single BRM system-of-record database across services, which introduces coupling. So architecturally it’s closer to CQRS with a 
shared database, and it has distributed-monolith risks. A more mature cloud-native microservice approach would move toward service-owned
data stores and event-driven integration

Is your system cloud-native: 
  Operationally, yes — we use microservices, async processing, and managed stores. But from a data-ownership standpoint, we still have a 
shared system-of-record database, which introduces coupling. So architecturally it’s a step toward cloud-native, but not fully aligned with
database-per-service principles.

What principles do you follow while designing cloud native applications? 
  When designing cloud-native systems, I follow a few core principles that align with how cloud environments behave.

  First, I design services to be stateless and horizontally scalable, so instances can be added or replaced without impact. Any state is 
  externalized to managed data stores.
  
  Second, I design for failure by default. I assume instances and dependencies can fail, so I use timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit
  breakers, and graceful degradation.
  
  Third, I focus on loose coupling and clear ownership. Services communicate through APIs or events and do not share databases or internal 
  state, which allows independent evolution.
  
  Fourth, I separate concerns like reads and writes when needed, often using event-driven patterns and eventual consistency to improve 
  scalability and resilience.
  
  Fifth, I rely heavily on automation—infrastructure as code, CI/CD pipelines, and immutable deployments—so the system is reproducible and 
  easy to operate.
  
  Finally, I treat observability as a first-class concern, with structured logging, metrics, tracing, and health checks, so the system can     be understood and debugged in production.
  
  Overall, the goal is to build systems that scale elastically, tolerate failure, and can evolve independently.”
  
Why eventual consistency is preferred in cloud-native design over strong consistency?
  Strong consistency requires co-ordination and synchronous communication between all the nodes and services, which will eventually increase
the latency.
  Whereas eventual consistency allows services to operate independently, communicate asynchronously. And system converges over time.
  That's said, it a deliberate trade-off. We use eventual consistency where temporary inconsistency is acceptable and reserve strong 
consistency for critical operations like payments, inventory management etc.

What do you mean by "System converges over time"? 
  Different parts of a distributed system may temporarily have different values, but all of them will eventually reflect the correct state.

  Example: 
  Order status update.
  1. Order status changes from CREATED to SHIPPED.
  2. Write service updates primary DB.
  3. Event is published.
  4. Caches, search indices update asynchronously.
  
  For a short time, DB says SHIPPED and Cache says CREATED. 
  After propagation, all say SHIPPED.

  Convergence assumes below: 
  - Reliable delivery (events, polling etc.)
  - Retry mechanisms
  - no conflicting writes.
  - Idempotent consumers
